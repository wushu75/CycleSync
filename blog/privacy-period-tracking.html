Privacy & Health Data

Your period data is more sensitive than your bank account. Here's why it matters who holds it.

Most period tracking apps store your most intimate health information on their servers. After 2022, the world learned exactly why that's a problem — and why we built CycleSync differently.

T
Tony MomohCreator of CycleSync · October 2026

In June 2022, something changed. Within days of a major legal ruling in the United States, privacy researchers and journalists started asking a question that millions of women hadn't thought to ask: where exactly does your period tracking app store your data?

The answer, for most apps, was: on their servers. In the US. Subject to US law. And potentially subject to legal requests from government bodies.

Flo, Clue, Natural Cycles, Period Tracker — these apps had collectively gathered the menstrual cycle data of hundreds of millions of women. Suddenly, that data wasn't just a health record. It was potential evidence.

What was at riskPeriod tracking data can reveal pregnancy, pregnancy loss, and fertility treatment. In jurisdictions where reproductive healthcare is restricted, that data could be subpoenaed, sold, or leaked — and used against the very women who trusted these apps with their health.

The problem isn't the app. It's the architecture.

Most period apps are built the same way: your data lives on their servers. That's not necessarily malicious — it makes syncing easy and enables AI-powered insights. But it means you are not in control of your own data. The company is.

When Flo Health settled with the FTC in 2021 for sharing user health data with Facebook and Google, they were doing what most app companies do: monetising data to fund the product. The difference is that period data is uniquely sensitive — and their users had no idea.

"I thought it was just an app on my phone. I didn't realise I was handing over my entire reproductive health history to a company I'd never heard of."

Local-first: what it means and why it changes everything

CycleSync was built on a different principle: your data never leaves your device. Everything you log is stored locally in your browser. It doesn't touch our servers. It doesn't pass through any third-party API.

There is no CycleSync database of your health records, because we never receive your health records.

What local-first means in practiceIf CycleSync were shut down tomorrow, your data would still be there — in your browser, under your control. If we were ever served with a legal request for user health data, we would have nothing to hand over. Not because we deleted it. Because we never had it.

Why I built it this way

I'm a UX designer. When I started building CycleSync, I could have stored data in the cloud, built a backend, grown a database of health records. I chose not to — because the women using this app are trusting it with something deeply personal.

The feedback that meant the most came from a user in Russia — someone I'll never meet, who wrote to say it mattered that their data stayed on their own device. That message changed how I thought about what I was building.

Privacy shouldn't be a premium feature

Some apps offer a "privacy mode" as a paid upgrade. Privacy isn't a feature — it's a right. CycleSync's free tier is private. The Pro tier adds features, not privacy. Your data stays local regardless of what you pay.
